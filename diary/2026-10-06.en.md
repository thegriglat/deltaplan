Yesterday's wind phase diagram idea was put to the test: about 8,200 solver runs on model mountains overnight, then analysis and a first prototype during the day. Outcome: the neural network is removed from the game; the wind field will be assembled by phases, and the Picard solver stays where it computes honestly and converges.

<!--more-->

## The idea
A physical-chemistry view: air over mountains has "states of matter" of the solution — wind crosses a ridge, gets blocked and flows around a mountain, separates into a rotor behind a steep crest, convection dominates on a hot day, cold air drains into valleys in the evening, and in a calm the field is not defined at all. Each regime gets its own way of computing, and the program first decides from terrain, wind and heating which regime is where ([research](/docs/research/air_phase.md), [architecture](/docs/research/air_phase_experts.md)).

The author asked directly: if this works, why keep a neural network in the game? The network was trained on Picard, and Picard fails exactly in calm, convection and evening conditions — the network inherited those gaps and hid them.

## Overnight runs
- One script runs all series with one command and resumes after interruption; the solver learned to compute 4 cases at once — twice as fast, larger batches do not help. Existing terrain was reused: the erosion generator was already there, ideal hill, ridge and escarpment shapes were added.
- Main run — 7,686 cases (Froude number grid, up-and-down sweep, under-relaxation, separation on a 100 m window, envelope, eroded terrain), about 5 GPU hours.
- **A plan flaw found overnight:** in the main grid Fr changed only through wind speed — Fr 0.1 meant 0.5 m/s, a calm, so blocking could not be told apart from calm. A 296-case series with fixed wind was added.
- **False alarm:** 89 % non-convergence in the first few hundred cases looked like a stratification override bug. A CPU check showed it was physics: all cases computed so far were calm, and the share matched the corpus (87 % vs 81–84 %).

## What the experiments showed
- Boundaries that matter to a pilot (rotors, lee zones, thermal sources) switch **sharply** around Fr ≈ 0.85–1.1.
- **No hysteresis:** a second start almost always reaches the same solution — no need to remember the history of the day.
- Most of Picard's "non-convergence" is a property of the iterations, not of the flow: under-relaxation (ω = 0.5) finds a solution and the field is almost the same. A steady field physically does not exist only in a true calm (near-ground wind up to 0.5 m/s).
- Thermal ceiling follows heating (−z_i/L), not Fr; flow separation behind a crest starts at slope 0.20–0.30 (literature: 0.27) and **disappears in daytime with heating above 100 W/m²** — rotors are stronger in the morning and evening.
- On eroded terrain the thresholds blur 5–10×: on real mountains transitions are wide.

## Prototype and decision
The first prototype assembled the field from phase formulas alone, without Picard. Thermals came out well (core strength differs from Picard in only 3 % of cases), speed is excellent (estimated 15–50 ms on GPU), but windward slope lift and lee zones were poor: linear theory is too smooth for them.

The author's decision: **drop the neural network entirely; phases are separate solutions for different regimes, and Picard handles some regimes perfectly well.** Final scheme: flow-over, slopes, separation and waves — Picard warm-started from the phase assembly; calm, convection, evening drainage and strong blocking — phase mechanisms; seams — by projection. Rejected: the network as a correction to the assembly (a black box again) and assembly alone without Picard (poor slopes).

Under-relaxation is also chosen by the phase map: ω = 0.5 right away near boundaries, ω = 1 in clean flow-over. The author asked why not always 0.5; the experiments answer that it is twice as slow on easy cases (211 iterations vs 111).

## Lee zone
The game's semi-empirical lee model (a "shadow line" from the crest plus a near-ground rotor) was calibrated from the experiments: rotor reverse flow 0.9 → 0.65, rotor height 0.4 → 0.6 of the drop, shear layer 60 → 350 m, sink 0.7 → 0.08 of the wind — the old value was 5–20× above measurements and literature. Danger behind a crest now comes from the rotor and turbulence, not from a sink hole. In daytime the rotor weakens with heating. For now it lives in the module branch and will reach the game together with the rest after a test flight.

## Ideas for later
- Blend phases with weights that minimize the equation residual rather than seam smoothness: real transitions can be sharp. Recorded in the module plan.
- If a crest with a "shadow line" is a pseudo-surface, Picard can treat it as a slope (an envelope): on real steep sites this cures non-convergence.
