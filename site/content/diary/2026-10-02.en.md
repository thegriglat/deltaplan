Version 1.0.2 came out overnight (the mouse always controls the wing, wind from the menu is measured above the launch). During the day a new direction for the air model was chosen — replace the in-game wind-field solver with a neural network trained on an honest offline solver. The "does the idea work" pilot was built, computed and analysed in one day: the network is clearly better than simple estimates but fell short of the targets. The main reason is that the network was trained on a different kind of terrain than the one it will work on. It was decided to repeat the pilot on 300 real mountain terrains.

## Network instead of solver: decisions

Currently the in-game wind field is computed by a Picard solver on the GPU: it takes several seconds at the "Computing wind from terrain…" stage, and the grid is limited by the time budget. The user's decisions ([plan](/docs/plan/air_nn.md)):
- there is no solver in the game — only the network (ONNX, a native Godot extension), run on the CPU;
- the "physics" of the field is an offline solver in Python + GPU with no limits on time or grid; the network is its compression;
- coverage — any point of the Earth's terrain; new physics (light absorption by the surface, humidity) is added via the offline solver and new network inputs;
- **the pilot comes first**: on the ready solver, check that the idea works at all, before any tooling;
- everything that can be done deterministically is done by a script; any step can be interrupted and resumed with the same command.

Along the way: a table of surface parameters by land-cover class (albedo, Bowen ratio, roughness) from the literature, and a probe of "ONNX Runtime inside Godot" — it works, but conflicts on symbols with the debug-drawing addon. The user's decision is to delete the addon entirely and redraw the F5 wind arrows with our own mesh.

## Pilot: what was computed

- Set: 1890 cases of the reference solver "as in the game" (38.4 km area, 400 m cell) — 4 built-in places, 5 synthetic shapes and 40 procedural terrains (ridges, hills, saddles, valleys with random sizes); about 7.5 h of GPU. 238 cases hit the iteration limit (this did not affect the network error).
- Network: a U-Net conditioned on the weather numbers (FiLM), 3.2 M parameters. Input — terrain map, heat flux, coordinates; the wind is rotated "from the west". Output — wind, lift and temperature at 13 heights above the terrain, as a correction to the inflow profile.
- A network pass on the CPU takes **19 ms** (4 threads), which is almost free compared with the budget.
- Validation — Ongudai entirely outside the training, 5 held-out procedural terrains, familiar places under new conditions, and a curve by the number of terrains in training (5/10/20/40).

## Pilot: results

| set | wind at 60 m, median | wind < 0.3 m/s | lift < 0.1 m/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| familiar terrain, new conditions | 0.47 m/s (inflow profile — 1.21) | 29 % | 92 % |
| held-out procedural | 0.63 m/s (profile — 1.75) | 21 % | 83 % |
| Ongudai | 0.87 m/s (profile — 1.12) | 12 % | 61 % |

The gate targets (90 % of points within 0.3 m/s for wind and 0.1 m/s for lift) were not met. Analysis:
- **The network learns, but there is too little data.** The validation error reaches a plateau by about epoch 30, while the training error keeps falling — 1188 cases on 44 terrains.
- **The curve by number of terrains falls for wind** (Ongudai 1.16 → 0.82 m/s at 5 → 40 terrains) — more terrains, more accurate wind. This is the main argument for the idea.
- **Ongudai is not noise but a systematic error:** in strong wind the network overestimates the speed over almost the whole area (in one case by 1.7 m/s on average, +14 %). The cause was found in the terrain: at a 400 m cell the typical slope of Ongudai is 0.22, of Altai 0.12, of Askarovo and Aushkul ~0.035, of the procedural ones 0.016. The network extrapolated beyond what it had seen.

## Decisions from the results

- **The wind criterion is relative** (Claude's proposal, accepted): error within `max(0.3 m/s; 10 %)`. The user: "nobody flies at +9 (and there ±1 m/s doesn't matter)". Besides, the reference itself — a solver on a 400 m grid — differs from nature by more than 10 %. Lift is kept strict (0.1 m/s): here it is not a 1–4 m/s thermal but the mean field, and tenths decide whether a slope holds and whether a thermal source switches on.
- **Do not change the normalisation.** The user suggested scaling everything to [0, 1]. Rejected: per-case normalisation erases scale (a 100 m hill and a 2 km ridge become identical), and common constants are the same linear transform as now and do not cure extrapolation. Instead, maps that are dimensionless by nature are added to the input: slope along and across the wind by the exact direction (near the ground lift ≈ wind × slope), position in the terrain (ridge, slope, valley) and openness to the wind.
- **Smooth or real terrains.** The user asked: why real terrain, and why not test the approximation on smooth ones? It turned out that in the game the input is always real Earth terrain through our loader, and the smooth procedural ones have already shown that the network works on a familiar class of terrain. The user's choice — **300 real terrains right away**, from mountains across all land, from gentle to very steep. The cutouts are prepared by the same path as the game input (the same tiles, smoothing and averaging), whole mountain systems are held out for validation. The procedural terrains already computed stay in the set.
- **The pipeline runs in batch stages** (the user): first download everything, then process everything in one run, then solver computation and training, parallelised across CPU and GPU. No "one place → look → next".

The module coordinator is preparing pilot 2: the estimate is one GPU night for ~3500 cases, area only.
